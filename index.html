Speech after a tongue-tie release: a first-month practice log

A tongue-tie release can change how the tongue moves; it does not automatically retrain speech sounds. This log is for the first four weeks after an adult or older-child release, when a speech-language pathologist has already set the exercises — or when you are preparing questions for that first therapy visit.

MedlinePlus describes a frenectomy in surgical terms as a procedure on the frenulum, the small fold of tissue being treated. The American Speech-Language-Hearing Association’s public page on ankyloglossia (tongue-tie) is the speech-profession’s patient starting point for how a restricted lingual frenulum can relate to speech, feeding and referral. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders explains speech and language as skills that can be assessed and treated by qualified professionals. None of those pages is a do-it-yourself protocol; the work below assumes you already have, or are about to get, a therapist’s plan.

What the speech guides on the site actually say

A page on speech therapy after an adult release is the place to read about why adults are often sent to therapy after the procedure rather than being told to “just talk more.” Pair it with the guide to tongue-tie and speech sounds, which is about which sounds are worth paying attention to, and with what speech therapists watch for — the cues a clinician uses in the room, which are more useful as questions than as self-scoring tools.

Use those three pages to fill the “sounds in focus” and “cues my therapist named” lines below, instead of inventing a generic list of letters.

Four-week log

Each block is one week. Short, daily sessions beat a single long weekend catch-up. If pain, bleeding, or a fever appears, stop and contact the clinician who did the release; this log is not aftercare for the wound.

Questions for the next visit
  1. Copy the therapist’s exercises in their words, not from memory after the appointment.
  2. Practise at a conversational speed only after the careful version is stable — the speech-sounds guide is about targets, not about rushing.
  3. If no therapist is in place yet, book one before you invent a programme. ASHA’s ankyloglossia page is a reasonable starting point for what that profession does; Frenectomy Guide helps you find release providers and the related therapy questions in one place.
This is not medical or speech-therapy advice and not a claim that a release will change speech on a set timetable. Follow the written plan from your own surgeon and speech-language pathologist.
